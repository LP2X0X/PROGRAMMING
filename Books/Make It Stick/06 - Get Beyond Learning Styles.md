# Get Beyond Learning Styles

> [!abstract] Overview
> Chapter 6 of [[00 - Make It Stick]] takes aim at one of the most beloved and most unfounded beliefs in education: that people have distinct "learning styles" (visual, auditory, kinesthetic) and that instruction should be matched to these styles. The authors present the evidence showing this theory is not supported by research, and then discuss what actually does differ between learners and what those differences mean for effective learning.

## The Learning Styles Hypothesis

The **learning styles hypothesis** comes in many forms, but the most popular version (often called the **VARK model**) claims:

- **Visual learners** learn best from images, diagrams, and spatial representations
- **Auditory learners** learn best from lectures, discussions, and verbal explanations
- **Reading/Writing learners** learn best from text-based materials
- **Kinesthetic learners** learn best from hands-on activities and physical manipulation

The hypothesis has two parts:
1. **People have a dominant learning style** (this is partly true -- people do have *preferences*)
2. **Matching instruction to a student's style improves learning** (this is not supported by evidence)

> [!danger] Critical Distinction
> Having a *preference* is not the same as having an *optimal learning channel*. You may *prefer* to learn from videos, but that does not mean you learn *better* from videos. The learning styles hypothesis makes a specific claim about matching instruction to preference improving outcomes, and ==this claim has been tested extensively and has not been supported==.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Learning styles (VARK: visual, auditory, reading/writing, kinesthetic) are widely believed
> - People do have learning *preferences* -- this is not disputed
> - The disputed claim: matching instruction to style improves learning
> - The evidence does not support the matching claim

## What the Evidence Actually Shows

### The Pashler Review (2008)

The most comprehensive review of learning styles research was conducted by Harold Pashler and colleagues. Their conclusions:

1. **Adequate evidence for learning styles would require a specific experimental design**: students classified by style, randomly assigned to instruction matched or mismatched to their style, then tested. If learning styles matter, there should be a significant interaction: visual learners should do better with visual instruction, auditory learners with auditory instruction, etc.

2. **Very few studies met this methodological standard**, and those that did ==consistently failed to find the predicted interaction==.

3. **The studies that claimed to support learning styles typically had fatal methodological flaws** -- confounding variables, no random assignment, or outcome measures that tested style-specific skills rather than actual learning.

### Why the Evidence Is Clear

- **Multiple well-designed studies** have tested the matching hypothesis
- **None have found** that matching instruction to learning style improves outcomes
- **Some studies found** that specific instructional methods are better for *everyone*, regardless of stated preference (e.g., active learning beats passive for all learners)

### What the Research Does Support

Instead of learning styles, the research supports:

- **Encoding variability**: using *multiple* modalities (seeing, hearing, doing, explaining) produces better learning for *everyone*
- **Matched to content**: some subjects are better taught with certain modalities (geometry benefits from visual representation; music benefits from auditory). This is about matching instruction to *content*, not to *student preference*
- **Individual differences in ability**: people do differ in verbal ability, spatial ability, working memory capacity, etc. These affect learning, but they are not "styles" -- they are abilities

> [!warning] Common Misconception
> **"Learning styles have been scientifically proven."** This is one of the most widespread misconceptions in education. Surveys show that 80-90% of teachers believe in learning styles, and many schools spend significant resources on learning style assessments and differentiated instruction. The scientific evidence does not support this practice. The resources would be better spent on strategies that *are* supported by evidence: retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, and elaboration.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - The Pashler review (2008) found no evidence for matching instruction to learning style
> - Well-designed studies consistently fail to find the predicted interaction
> - What works: multi-modal encoding (for everyone), matching instruction to content, and evidence-based strategies
> - 80-90% of teachers believe in learning styles despite the lack of evidence

## Why the Myth Persists

The authors discuss several reasons learning styles remain popular despite the evidence:

### 1. It Feels True

People *do* have preferences, and preferences feel like insights about how you learn best. The subjective experience of preference is real; the conclusion that preference equals optimal channel is wrong.

### 2. It Is Flattering

Being told "you are a visual learner" feels like a useful insight about yourself. It provides a label and a framework. People like to categorize themselves and feel understood.

### 3. It Provides an Excuse

If you struggle with a lecture, you can attribute it to a mismatch between the lecture format and your learning style rather than to insufficient effort or poor study strategies. Learning styles externalize the cause of difficulty.

### 4. Commercial Interests

There is a large industry built around learning style assessments, differentiated instruction materials, and professional development programs. This industry has financial incentives to promote the concept.

### 5. Kernel of Truth

People *do* differ in ability profiles (stronger verbal vs. spatial skills), and some content *is* better taught in certain modalities. These real phenomena are incorrectly attributed to learning styles.

> [!tip] How to Respond When Someone Invokes Learning Styles
> Rather than arguing against learning styles (which can feel like a personal attack), redirect to what the evidence *does* support:
> - "The research shows that everyone learns better with multi-modal practice -- seeing, doing, explaining. So let's use all of those."
> - "The most effective strategies -- like practice testing and spacing -- work for all learners regardless of preference."

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Learning styles persist because they feel true, are flattering, provide an excuse, and have commercial backing
> - The real phenomena (preferences, ability differences, content-modality matching) are misattributed to learning styles
> - The most productive response is redirecting to evidence-based strategies that work for everyone

## What Actually Differs Between Learners

While learning styles are unsupported, the authors acknowledge that learners *do* differ in meaningful ways:

### 1. Prior Knowledge

The single strongest predictor of how well you will learn new material is **what you already know about the topic**. New information is encoded by connecting it to existing knowledge. More prior knowledge means more hooks for new information to attach to.

### 2. Ability Profiles

People genuinely differ in:
- **Verbal ability** -- facility with language and text
- **Spatial ability** -- facility with visual-spatial reasoning
- **Working memory capacity** -- how much information can be held and manipulated simultaneously
- **Processing speed** -- how quickly information can be processed

These are real cognitive differences, but they are *abilities*, not *styles*. And importantly, they can be developed with practice.

### 3. Motivation and Mindset

See [[07 - Increase Your Abilities]] for the role of **growth mindset** (believing abilities can be developed through effort) vs. **fixed mindset** (believing abilities are innate and unchangeable).

### 4. Self-Regulation

The ability to plan, monitor, and adjust your learning strategies. This is essentially metacognition (see [[05 - Avoid Illusions of Knowing]]) combined with executive function.

### 5. Interest and Curiosity

Genuine interest in a topic enhances attention, encoding, and persistence. Interest is not a "style" -- it is an emotional state that improves all learning processes.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Learners differ meaningfully in prior knowledge, abilities, motivation, self-regulation, and interest
> - These are real differences, but they are not "learning styles"
> - Prior knowledge is the strongest predictor of future learning
> - Abilities can be developed; they are not fixed categories

## What Does Matter for Learning

Instead of matching instruction to (nonexistent) learning styles, the evidence supports:

### 1. Match Instruction to Content

Teach spatial concepts spatially. Teach auditory skills auditorily. The match should be between *content and modality*, not between *student and modality*.

### 2. Use Multiple Modalities

Everyone benefits from encoding information through multiple channels. Read it, hear it, discuss it, draw it, teach it, practice it. ==More encoding channels mean more retrieval cues.==

### 3. Focus on Evidence-Based Strategies

The strategies that work for everyone, regardless of preference:
- [[To Learn, Retrieve|Retrieval practice]]
- [[Mix Up Your Practice|Spaced and interleaved practice]]
- [[Embrace Difficulties|Elaboration and generation]]
- [[Avoid Illusions of Knowing|Calibration]]

### 4. Develop Metacognition

Teach learners to monitor and regulate their own learning. This skill multiplies the effectiveness of every other strategy.

### 5. Build on Prior Knowledge

Assess what learners already know and build from there. The most effective instruction connects new material to existing knowledge structures.

> [!tip] The Takeaway for Self-Learners
> Stop worrying about "what type of learner you are." Instead:
> 1. Use retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving (these work for everyone)
> 2. Engage with material through multiple channels (read, write, discuss, practice)
> 3. Focus on connecting new material to what you already know
> 4. Develop your metacognitive skills so you can accurately assess and direct your learning

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Match instruction to content, not to student preference
> - Use multiple modalities for richer encoding (works for everyone)
> - Evidence-based strategies (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, elaboration) transcend individual differences
> - Metacognition and prior knowledge are the real differentiators

## Comprehensive Summary

> [!tip] Complete Summary
> Chapter 6 dismantles the learning styles myth and replaces it with what the evidence actually supports. The key insight: ==people have learning *preferences*, but matching instruction to those preferences does not improve learning outcomes==. Multiple well-designed studies have tested the learning styles hypothesis and failed to find support for it.
>
> The myth persists because it feels intuitively true (we do have preferences), is flattering (it gives us a label), provides an excuse for difficulty (mismatched instruction rather than insufficient effort), and has commercial backing (a large industry of assessments and materials).
>
> What actually differs between learners is meaningful but different from "styles": prior knowledge (the strongest predictor), ability profiles (verbal, spatial, working memory), motivation and mindset, self-regulation, and interest. These are real differences, but they are abilities and states, not fixed categories.
>
> What actually improves learning for everyone: matching instruction to *content* (not student preference), using multiple modalities (more encoding channels = more retrieval cues), applying evidence-based strategies (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, elaboration), developing metacognition, and building on prior knowledge.
>
> The practical message: stop trying to identify your learning style and start using the strategies that work for all learners.
