# Make It Stick - The Science of Successful Learning

> [!abstract] Overview
> "Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning" by Peter C. Brown, Henry L. Roediger III, and Mark A. McDaniel is a landmark book grounded in decades of cognitive psychology research. It dismantles popular but ineffective study habits -- rereading, highlighting, massed practice -- and replaces them with evidence-based strategies that produce durable, flexible learning. The core message: ==learning that feels easy is often shallow; learning that feels effortful is what actually sticks==.

## About the Authors

- **Peter C. Brown** -- a novelist and management consultant who translates the research into accessible narrative
- **Henry L. Roediger III** -- a cognitive psychologist at Washington University in St. Louis, one of the world's foremost experts on human memory and the testing effect
- **Mark A. McDaniel** -- a cognitive psychologist also at Washington University, whose research focuses on prospective memory and educational applications of cognitive science

The unique strength of this book is the marriage of rigorous empirical research (Roediger and McDaniel have published hundreds of peer-reviewed papers) with readable storytelling (Brown).

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Written by two leading cognitive scientists and a skilled writer
> - Grounded in peer-reviewed research spanning decades
> - Bridges the gap between laboratory findings and practical application

## Core Thesis

The book's central argument rests on a counterintuitive truth:

> **The most effective learning strategies feel harder and slower than the ones most people use, but they produce deeper, more durable knowledge.**

Most learners gravitate toward strategies that create **illusions of competence** -- rereading makes text feel familiar, massed practice creates short-term fluency -- but these methods produce fragile learning that fades quickly.

The strategies that actually work -- retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, elaboration, generation -- introduce **desirable difficulties** that force the brain to reconstruct knowledge, strengthening the neural pathways each time.

> [!warning] Common Misconception
> "If it feels easy, I must be learning well." The opposite is closer to the truth. Fluency of processing (how easily material flows) is a poor indicator of actual learning. The effort of retrieval, the struggle of spacing, the confusion of interleaving -- these are *signs that learning is happening*, not signs that it is failing.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Effective learning feels harder but produces durable results
> - Popular study methods create illusions of competence
> - "Desirable difficulties" are the engine of deep learning
> - Fluency is not a reliable signal of mastery

## The Seven Evidence-Based Learning Strategies

These are the seven core strategies the book advocates, each supported by extensive research:

### 1. Retrieval Practice
See: [[02 - To Learn, Retrieve]]

Testing yourself on material -- not as assessment, but as a *learning strategy*. Every time you retrieve a memory, you strengthen it and make it more accessible in the future.

### 2. Spaced Practice
See: [[03 - Mix Up Your Practice]]

Distributing study sessions over time rather than cramming. The forgetting that happens between sessions is not a bug -- it is a feature. Relearning after partial forgetting reconsolidates memory more strongly.

### 3. Interleaving
See: [[03 - Mix Up Your Practice]]

Mixing different topics, problem types, or skills within a single study session rather than practicing one thing until you "master" it before moving on.

### 4. Elaboration
See: [[04 - Embrace Difficulties]]

Explaining new material in your own words and connecting it to what you already know. The richer the web of associations, the more retrieval cues you create.

### 5. Generation
See: [[04 - Embrace Difficulties]]

Attempting to produce an answer or solve a problem *before* being shown the solution. Even wrong attempts prime the brain to encode the correct answer more deeply.

### 6. Reflection
See: [[04 - Embrace Difficulties]]

A combination of retrieval and elaboration -- looking back at what you learned, asking what went well, what could improve, and what you would do differently. This is metacognition in action.

### 7. Calibration
See: [[05 - Avoid Illusions of Knowing]]

Aligning your self-assessment of what you know with objective reality. Using tools like practice tests, peer review, and external feedback to combat the illusion of knowing.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Seven strategies form the book's actionable framework
> - All are grounded in cognitive science, not pop psychology
> - They work because they exploit how memory encoding and retrieval actually function
> - Each strategy creates "desirable difficulty" that strengthens learning

## Key Themes Across the Book

### Effort is Not the Enemy -- It Is the Point
The book consistently argues that ==struggle and difficulty during learning are features, not bugs==. When you have to work to retrieve something, you strengthen the retrieval pathway. When you have to generate an answer before seeing it, you encode it more deeply.

### Mental Models Matter More Than Facts
Learning is not about stockpiling isolated facts. It is about building **mental models** -- internal representations of how things work, how concepts relate, and how to apply knowledge in novel situations. The strategies in this book all contribute to building richer, more flexible mental models.

### Metacognition Is a Learnable Skill
Many people are poor judges of what they know and do not know. The book treats metacognition -- the ability to accurately assess your own knowledge -- as a skill that can and must be developed. Without it, you cannot direct your learning effectively.

### Empirical Evidence Over Intuition
The authors repeatedly contrast what *feels* right with what the evidence *shows*. This is a book that trusts data over dogma, even when the data contradicts deeply held beliefs (such as learning styles).

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Effort during learning signals that encoding is happening
> - Mental models, not isolated facts, are the goal of learning
> - Metacognition is trainable and essential for effective self-directed learning
> - The book's authority rests on empirical evidence, not opinion

## Practical Applications

### For Students / Self-Learners
- Replace rereading with self-quizzing after each study session
- Space your study: review material at increasing intervals
- Interleave different subjects or problem types within a session
- After reading a chapter, close the book and write down everything you can remember
- Use flashcards with spaced repetition (e.g., [[Anki]])
- Before looking up a solution, try to solve the problem yourself first

### For Teachers / Trainers
- Use frequent low-stakes quizzes (not for grading, but for learning)
- Design curricula that revisit earlier material at spaced intervals
- Mix problem types on homework and tests
- Encourage students to struggle before providing answers
- Teach students about the science of learning itself

### For Professionals / Lifelong Learners
- After meetings or training, write a reflection on key takeaways
- Use the "generation effect" when learning new skills -- try before you study
- Periodically quiz yourself on material from past months
- Seek feedback and external calibration regularly

> [!tip] The Single Most Powerful Habit
> If you adopt only one strategy from this book, make it **retrieval practice**. After every learning session, close your materials and try to recall everything you just learned. This single habit, done consistently, will transform your retention.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Strategies are applicable to students, teachers, and professionals alike
> - The simplest starting point is retrieval practice after every session
> - Spaced repetition tools like Anki operationalize these principles
> - Teaching others is itself a form of retrieval and elaboration

## Comprehensive Summary

> [!tip] Complete Summary
> "Make It Stick" is a book that rewrites the rules of learning based on what cognitive science has proven, not what tradition or intuition suggests. Its core message: **the learning strategies that feel most productive are often the least effective, and the strategies that feel most difficult are the most powerful.**
>
> The book demolishes the myth that rereading, highlighting, and massed (crammed) practice lead to lasting learning. These methods create **fluency illusions** -- the material *feels* familiar, so we believe we know it, but this familiarity evaporates quickly.
>
> In their place, the authors present seven evidence-based strategies:
>
> 1. **Retrieval practice** -- testing yourself to strengthen memory traces
> 2. **Spaced practice** -- distributing learning over time so that forgetting and relearning consolidate knowledge
> 3. **Interleaving** -- mixing topics and problem types to build discrimination and transfer
> 4. **Elaboration** -- explaining material in your own words and connecting it to prior knowledge
> 5. **Generation** -- attempting answers before seeing solutions to prime deeper encoding
> 6. **Reflection** -- combining retrieval with elaboration by reviewing what you've learned and how
> 7. **Calibration** -- using objective feedback to align self-assessment with reality
>
> The book also dismantles the popular "learning styles" myth (visual, auditory, kinesthetic), showing that while people have preferences, matching instruction to preference does not improve outcomes. What does matter is the *type of encoding* -- deep, effortful, multi-modal processing.
>
> A major theme is **desirable difficulties** (a term coined by Robert Bjork): conditions that make learning harder in the moment but stronger in the long run. Spacing creates forgetting that must be overcome. Interleaving creates confusion that forces discrimination. Generation creates errors that prime correction. These difficulties are not obstacles to learning -- they *are* learning.
>
> The book emphasizes that learning is fundamentally about building **mental models** -- rich, interconnected representations that allow you to recognize patterns, make predictions, and transfer knowledge to new situations. The recommended strategies all serve this goal.
>
> Finally, the book stresses **metacognition** -- knowing what you know and what you don't. Most people are poor self-assessors, and this leads them to study the wrong things, stop too early, or use ineffective methods. Calibration through testing, feedback, and honest self-reflection is the antidote.
>
> The practical upshot: replace passive review with active retrieval, space your practice, mix it up, elaborate on everything, generate before consuming, reflect regularly, and calibrate ruthlessly. Learning will feel harder. It will also actually work.
