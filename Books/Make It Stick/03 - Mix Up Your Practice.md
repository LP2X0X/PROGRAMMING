# Mix Up Your Practice

> [!abstract] Overview
> Chapter 3 of [[00 - Make It Stick]] covers two of the most powerful (and most counterintuitive) learning strategies: **spaced practice** and **interleaving**. Both feel less effective in the moment than their conventional alternatives (cramming and blocked practice), but they produce dramatically superior long-term retention and transfer. The chapter also introduces **varied practice** -- changing the conditions of practice to build more flexible knowledge.

## Spaced Practice

> [!info] Definition
> **Spaced practice** (also called **distributed practice**) is the strategy of spreading study sessions over time rather than concentrating them in a single session (massed practice / cramming). Instead of studying a topic for 3 hours in one sitting, you study it for 1 hour across 3 sessions separated by days or weeks.

### The Core Principle

The spacing effect is one of the oldest and most replicated findings in cognitive psychology, dating back to Hermann Ebbinghaus's experiments in 1885.

**Key finding**: For the same total amount of study time, spaced practice produces dramatically better long-term retention than massed practice.

### How Spacing Works

```mermaid
graph LR
    A[Study Session 1] -->|Time gap: partial forgetting| B[Study Session 2]
    B -->|Time gap: partial forgetting| C[Study Session 3]
    C -->|Time gap: partial forgetting| D[Study Session 4]
    style A fill:#4a9eff,color:#fff
    style B fill:#4a9eff,color:#fff
    style C fill:#4a9eff,color:#fff
    style D fill:#4a9eff,color:#fff
```

Between sessions, you *forget some of what you learned*. This forgetting is not a failure -- ==it is the mechanism that makes spacing work==. When you relearn material after a period of forgetting:

1. **The retrieval is more effortful** -- creating a desirable difficulty
2. **Reconsolidation strengthens the memory** -- the memory is re-encoded more robustly
3. **New connections are formed** -- you now have associations from multiple study contexts
4. **The forgetting curve flattens** -- each cycle of forget-and-retrieve makes the memory more resistant to future forgetting

### Optimal Spacing Intervals

The research suggests expanding intervals:

| Review Number | Suggested Interval |
|---------------|-------------------|
| 1st review | 1-2 days after initial learning |
| 2nd review | 1 week later |
| 3rd review | 2-4 weeks later |
| 4th review | 1-2 months later |
| Subsequent | Progressively longer |

> [!tip] The Practical Rule
> The optimal spacing interval depends on when you need the knowledge. A rough guideline: ==the interval between practice sessions should be 10-30% of the period over which you want to retain the information==. Want to remember something for a year? Space your reviews weeks apart. Want to remember for a week? Space them a day apart.

> [!warning] Common Misconception
> **"Spacing wastes time because I have to re-learn what I forgot."** The re-learning IS the point. Each re-learning episode strengthens the memory more than the original learning did. The "wasted" time (forgetting) is actually the mechanism that makes the next retrieval more effective.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Spaced practice distributes learning across time with deliberate gaps
> - Forgetting between sessions is productive -- it creates desirable difficulty
> - Each retrieve-after-forgetting cycle makes memory more durable
> - Expanding intervals (1 day, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month...) work well
> - The spacing effect has been replicated for over 140 years

## The Spacing Effect -- Why Forgetting Is Productive

The chapter dives deeper into *why* forgetting is not just acceptable but necessary for strong learning.

### The New Theory of Disuse (Bjork & Bjork)

Robert and Elizabeth Bjork proposed that every memory has two properties:

- **Storage strength** -- how well the memory is encoded (does not decrease over time)
- **Retrieval strength** -- how easily the memory can be accessed (decreases over time without use)

**Key insight**: When retrieval strength is *low* (you've partially forgotten) but storage strength is *high* (the memory exists), retrieving the memory produces a **large gain in both storage and retrieval strength**. This is why spacing works -- the gap allows retrieval strength to drop, making the subsequent retrieval maximally effective.

> [!example] The Muscle Analogy
> Spacing works like strength training. Lifting weights tears muscle fibers (analogous to the "damage" of forgetting). Rest allows the fibers to rebuild stronger (analogous to reconsolidation). Lifting every hour without rest produces no growth and leads to injury. The rest between sessions is not idle time -- it is when the growth happens.

### Consolidation and Sleep

The authors note that sleep plays a critical role in memory consolidation. During sleep, the brain:
- Replays the day's learning experiences
- Transfers information from hippocampus (short-term) to neocortex (long-term)
- Prunes weak connections and strengthens strong ones
- Integrates new knowledge with existing mental models

This is another reason spacing works -- it gives the brain time to consolidate between sessions, including at least one sleep cycle.

> [!tip] Study Timing Implication
> Studying before sleep is particularly effective because the material gets first priority in overnight consolidation. The ideal daily routine: study new material in the evening, sleep, then do a brief retrieval practice the next morning to capitalize on the overnight consolidation.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Bjork's New Theory of Disuse: storage strength vs. retrieval strength
> - Low retrieval strength + high storage strength = maximum learning from retrieval
> - Spacing allows retrieval strength to dip, making subsequent retrieval more effective
> - Sleep consolidation is a critical part of the spacing mechanism
> - Studying before sleep + retrieving the next morning is an ideal pattern

## Interleaving

> [!info] Definition
> **Interleaving** is the practice of mixing different topics, problem types, or skills within a single study session, rather than completing all practice on one topic before moving to the next (blocked practice).

### Blocked vs. Interleaved

- **Blocked practice**: AAAA BBBB CCCC (master one topic, then move to the next)
- **Interleaved practice**: ABCA BCAB CABC (mix topics throughout)

### Why Interleaving Works

Interleaving produces its benefits through several mechanisms:

1. **Discrimination** -- When you see different problem types side by side, you must learn to *identify* which type you are facing and *select* the appropriate strategy. Blocked practice never requires this because you always know what type of problem is coming next.

2. **Retrieval from different contexts** -- Switching topics requires reloading the relevant knowledge, which is itself a retrieval event. Each reload strengthens the memory.

3. **Preventing "autopilot"** -- Blocked practice quickly becomes mindless repetition. Interleaving forces you to stay engaged because the context keeps changing.

4. **Building transfer skills** -- In the real world, problems do not come labeled by type. Interleaving trains you to recognize and classify problems -- a critical real-world skill.

> [!example] The Baseball Batting Study
> In a famous study, baseball players practiced hitting three types of pitches:
> - **Blocked group**: 15 fastballs, then 15 curveballs, then 15 changeups
> - **Interleaved group**: 45 pitches in random order
>
> During practice, the blocked group performed better (higher hit rate). But in a subsequent game-like test with random pitches, the **interleaved group performed significantly better**. They had learned to *read* the pitch, not just hit a known pitch type.

> [!danger] Critical Insight
> Interleaving produces a paradox that misleads both students and teachers: ==performance *during practice* is worse with interleaving, but performance *on later tests* is better==. This means both students and teachers often conclude that interleaving is not working -- precisely because it IS working (creating desirable difficulties that strengthen learning).

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Interleaving mixes topics/problem types within a session
> - It builds discrimination: the ability to identify problem type and select strategy
> - Real-world problems come unlabeled; interleaving trains real-world readiness
> - Practice performance is worse (misleading), but test/transfer performance is better
> - Both students and teachers are fooled by the poor practice performance

## Blocked vs. Interleaved Practice -- The Evidence

### The Kornell and Bjork Painting Study (2008)

Students learned to identify the painting styles of 12 artists. One group studied each artist's paintings in blocks (6 paintings by Artist A, then 6 by Artist B...). The other group saw the paintings interleaved.

**Results on a test with new paintings by the same artists:**
- Interleaved group: **significantly better** at identifying artists
- Blocked group: performed worse despite feeling more confident

This is important because the test required *transfer* -- recognizing style in paintings they had never seen. Interleaving trained them to extract the underlying patterns, not just memorize specific examples.

### The Rohrer and Taylor Math Study (2007)

Students practiced calculating volumes of four different geometric shapes.
- Blocked group: practiced all problems of one shape, then the next
- Interleaved group: practiced problems of different shapes mixed together

**Results on a test one week later:**
- Blocked group: **20% correct**
- Interleaved group: **63% correct**

> [!danger] Read That Again
> The interleaved group scored more than **three times** as well as the blocked group. This was not a subtle effect. And yet, during practice, the blocked group performed better and reported feeling more confident.

### Why Do Students and Teachers Prefer Blocked Practice?

1. **It feels more productive** -- you get into a "groove" and can solve problems quickly
2. **Performance during practice is higher** -- which is mistaken for learning
3. **It satisfies the desire for mastery before moving on** -- "I want to nail this before tackling something new"
4. **Curricula are typically organized this way** -- Chapter 3 problems all relate to Chapter 3

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Studies consistently show interleaving produces 2-3x better retention and transfer
> - The effect is robust across domains: art, math, sports, medicine
> - Blocked practice creates an illusion of mastery through artificially inflated practice performance
> - Students and teachers both prefer blocked practice, misled by its in-session effectiveness

## Varied Practice

> [!info] Definition
> **Varied practice** means practicing a skill under different conditions, contexts, or parameters rather than repeating it identically each time. This is related to interleaving but focuses on *how* you practice one skill rather than *which* skills you mix.

### Examples

- A basketball player practices free throws from slightly different spots on the line, not just the exact same position
- A student solves the same type of equation but with different numbers, contexts, and framings
- A programmer implements the same algorithm in different languages or with different constraints

### Why It Works

Varied practice builds **flexible, transferable knowledge** by:
- Extracting the *underlying principles* rather than memorizing a specific instance
- Creating a broader base of retrieval cues
- Preparing you for the variability of real-world application

> [!tip] For Programmers
> Do not just practice one solution to a problem. Implement it multiple ways. Change the constraints. Solve similar problems with different data structures. This builds the flexible mental models that allow you to adapt when real-world problems do not match textbook examples exactly.

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Varied practice changes the conditions of practice, not just the topics
> - It extracts underlying principles rather than memorizing specific instances
> - Creates broader retrieval cues and more flexible knowledge
> - Complements interleaving by adding variability within each skill

## Why These Strategies Feel Wrong

This section explains the psychological reasons that spacing and interleaving feel less effective:

### The Illusion of Fluency

Massed, blocked practice creates a feeling of **fluency** -- the material comes to mind quickly and easily. But this fluency is tied to the *current context* (you just studied it) and dissipates rapidly. Spacing and interleaving feel disfluent, which the brain interprets as "I'm not learning."

### The Metacognitive Error

People judge their learning by **ease of processing** during study. Since massed/blocked practice is easier, they rate their learning as higher. Since spaced/interleaved practice is harder, they rate their learning as lower. ==Both judgments are wrong.==

### The Performance vs. Learning Distinction

This is one of the book's most important distinctions:

- **Performance** -- how well you can do something *right now, during practice*
- **Learning** -- how well you can do something *later, in a different context*

Massed/blocked practice maximizes performance. Spaced/interleaved practice maximizes learning. They are not the same thing, and optimizing for one comes at the cost of the other.

> [!warning] Common Misconception
> **"If I can do it now, I've learned it."** Performance during practice is a *terrible* predictor of long-term learning. You can perform well during massed practice because the information is fresh in working memory, not because it has been encoded into long-term memory. The real test is always: can you do it tomorrow? Next week? In a novel context?

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Massed/blocked practice creates fluency illusions
> - People judge learning by ease of processing, which is misleading
> - Performance (now) and learning (later) are different constructs
> - Optimizing for current performance often undermines actual learning

## How to Implement Spacing and Interleaving

### Practical Spacing Strategies

1. **Create a study calendar** -- map out when you will revisit each topic, with expanding intervals
2. **Use spaced repetition software** -- [[Anki]], Mnemosyne, or similar tools automate optimal spacing
3. **Review yesterday's material before today's** -- a simple daily habit that introduces spacing
4. **Maintain a "review queue"** -- keep a running list of topics to revisit at intervals

### Practical Interleaving Strategies

1. **Mix problem types in practice sessions** -- do not complete all of one type before starting another
2. **Create mixed practice sets** -- after learning several topics, create practice sheets that blend them
3. **Study multiple subjects in one session** -- 30 minutes of math, 30 minutes of history, 30 minutes of programming
4. **Use textbook problems out of order** -- do not just complete the end-of-chapter exercises for the chapter you just read; mix in problems from earlier chapters

### The Combined Power

The most effective protocol combines all three strategies:

1. **Space** your study sessions over time
2. **Interleave** different topics within each session
3. **Vary** the conditions of practice
4. **Retrieve** rather than reread (from [[02 - To Learn, Retrieve]])

> [!tip] A Practical Weekly Schedule
> | Day | Activity |
> |-----|----------|
> | Monday | Study Topic A (new). Retrieve Topic C (from last week). |
> | Tuesday | Study Topic B (new). Retrieve Topic A (from yesterday). |
> | Wednesday | Interleave practice: mixed problems from A, B, and C. |
> | Thursday | Study Topic C-2 (new). Retrieve Topics A and B. |
> | Friday | Mixed review: retrieve and interleave all topics. |
> | Weekend | Reflect on the week. Free recall on everything. |

> [!summary] Section Summary
> - Spacing requires a study calendar or spaced repetition software
> - Interleaving requires mixing problem types and subjects within sessions
> - The most effective approach combines spacing, interleaving, varied practice, and retrieval
> - A structured weekly schedule can operationalize all of these

## Comprehensive Summary

> [!tip] Complete Summary
> Chapter 3 presents two of the most powerful and most counterintuitive learning strategies: **spaced practice** and **interleaving**, along with **varied practice** as a complementary approach.
>
> **Spaced practice** distributes study across time with deliberate gaps. The forgetting that occurs between sessions is not a problem -- it is the engine of the strategy. When you retrieve knowledge after partial forgetting, the retrieval is effortful (desirable difficulty), and the subsequent reconsolidation produces a stronger, more durable memory trace. Each cycle of forget-retrieve-reconsolidate flattens the forgetting curve. The spacing effect has been replicated for over 140 years and is one of the most robust findings in cognitive psychology.
>
> **Interleaving** mixes different topics, problem types, or skills within a single study session. It feels harder and produces worse practice performance, but generates dramatically better retention and transfer. The key mechanism is **discrimination** -- interleaving forces you to identify what type of problem you are facing and select the appropriate strategy, which blocked practice never requires. Studies consistently show 2-3x better test performance with interleaving.
>
> **Varied practice** changes the conditions under which you practice a single skill, building more flexible and transferable knowledge by extracting underlying principles rather than memorizing specific instances.
>
> The central challenge of this chapter is that ==all three strategies feel less effective in the moment==. Massed, blocked, repetitive practice creates a fluency illusion that misleads both students and teachers. The book draws a critical distinction between **performance** (how well you do right now) and **learning** (how well you do later, in a different context). These strategies sacrifice short-term performance to maximize long-term learning.
